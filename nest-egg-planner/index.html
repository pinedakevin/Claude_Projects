How to use

Set up your plan in about 15 minutes

Have your bank app, a recent pay stub, and your retirement account statements nearby. You can change anything later, and every change updates your weeks and your retirement projection right away.

  1. Start fresh. The planner opens with an example household. Tap Start blank in the banner, or type over the example numbers.
  2. Right now. On Your money, enter what's in checking and in your emergency fund today, and set an emergency fund goal (many people aim for three to six months of expenses).
  3. Income. Add each paycheck as take-home pay, how often it comes, and the next date it lands.
  4. Bills. Add everything with a set amount and due day: rent or mortgage, car payment, utilities, insurance, subscriptions.
  5. Flexible spending. Set a monthly amount for things that vary. The total is your real limit; each week gets total × 12 ÷ 52.
  6. Accounts. Add every account you already have: 401(k), IRA, HSA, brokerage, and savings, CDs or money market accounts, with today's balance and monthly contributions. Old accounts you no longer add to get $0 a month. Mark whether money comes out of your paycheck or from checking, and add any employer match. Savings-type accounts get their own interest rate.
  7. About you. Enter your age, your Social Security estimate, and any pension. Leave retirement spending blank to use what you spend today.
  8. Your two plans. Plan A and Plan B let you compare. Each sets a monthly amount for the emergency fund, a checking cushion, how much of your account contributions to make (100% is what you entered), what to do with leftover cash, and the age you retire.
  9. Read your weeks. Week by week shows each week's money in and out, what goes to savings and investing, and where checking ends up. Tap a week for every number.
  10. Look ahead. Retirement shows your savings by age, your nest egg at retirement, monthly income in retirement, and whether the money lasts.
  11. Back it up. Your plan lives only in this browser. Tap Backup now and then to save a backup file or a spreadsheet (.csv).

How each week is figured

Checking at the start of the week+ money that lands this week− bills due− investing from checking (on its day)− transfer to your emergency fund− your weekly flexible spending− unexpected charges= what's left this week

If a plan invests leftover cash, it first holds back enough for the next few weeks' bills (3 by default) and keeps your checking cushion, then invests the rest. Otherwise the leftover stays in checking.

How retirement is projected

  • Each investment account grows at the yearly return you pick (5%, 7% or 10%), and savings, CD and money market accounts grow at their own interest rate, with your monthly contributions until the age you retire. Contributions rise with your yearly raise.
  • After you retire, you draw what you need each month: your retirement spending, minus Social Security and any pension once they start. All three rise with inflation.
  • "Lasts to" is the age when savings would run out at that pace.
  • Today's dollars shows future amounts in today's buying power, which is easier to compare to what things cost now.

Common questions

What's the difference between the two plans?

Only the settings you give them. A typical setup: Plan A is what you do today; Plan B contributes more (say 150%), invests leftover cash each week, or retires at a different age. Compare them on Retirement.

If I contribute more from my paycheck, why does my take-home drop by the full amount here?

To keep things simple, extra paycheck contributions lower take-home dollar for dollar. Pre-tax contributions actually lower it a bit less, because you pay less income tax.

Why doesn't my employer match go up when I contribute more?

Most matches have a cap, like 100% of the first 3% of pay. The planner keeps the match you entered. If yours would grow, raise the employer amount on that account.

Does it include taxes in retirement?

No. Money from Traditional (pre-tax) accounts is taxed when you take it out, while Roth and most HSA withdrawals aren't. Where the money sits shows the split so you can plan for it.

Can I keep my plan in a spreadsheet?

Yes. Backup → Save spreadsheet (.csv) gives you your inputs, every week's budget for both plans, and your retirement savings by age. Edit the inputs in the first table and load the file back with Load a backup. Keep the first column (Section) and the column names as they are.

Who can see my numbers?

Only you, in this browser. Nothing you enter is sent anywhere. Clearing your browser data erases it, so keep a backup.

How accurate is it?

It's an estimate. Markets don't return the same amount every year, and pay, prices and rules change. Use it to compare choices and see the direction, and update your numbers as life changes.

Retirement 101

The retirement accounts, explained plainly

Retirement accounts are ordinary investment accounts with tax breaks attached. The trade for those breaks is rules: yearly limits on how much you put in, and penalties for taking money out early. Knowing a few account types and two tax "flavors" covers most of what you need.

Dollar limits below are the IRS figures for 2026. The IRS usually announces next year's limits in November, so check for updates each year.

Why starting early matters

Money invested grows, and then the growth grows too (compounding). Time does most of the work, so small amounts started early can beat bigger amounts started late. Try it:

Two tax flavors: Traditional and Roth

Most accounts come in one or both flavors. The difference is when you pay income tax.

Traditional (pre-tax)Roth (after-tax)
Going inLowers your taxable income nowNo tax break now
While investedGrows without yearly taxesGrows without yearly taxes
Coming out in retirementTaxed as incomeTax-free, if the rules are met
Tends to fit whenYour tax rate now is higher than you expect laterYour tax rate now is lower than you expect later, or you're early in your career

Many people split between both so they have some taxable and some tax-free money to draw from later.

Through your job

401(k), 403(b), 457(b), TSP

Plans your employer offers: a 401(k) at companies, a 403(b) at schools, hospitals and nonprofits, a 457(b) at state and local governments, and the TSP for federal workers and the military. Money comes straight out of your paycheck.

  • 2026 limit: $24,500 of your own pay. Age 50+ can add $8,000 more; ages 60–63 can add $11,250 instead.
  • Employer match: many employers add money when you contribute, for example 100% of the first 3% of your pay. That's an instant return, which is why "get the full match" is common first advice.
  • Vesting: your own contributions are always yours. Employer money may "vest" over a few years, meaning you keep it only after staying that long.
  • Changing jobs: you can leave it, move it to the new job's plan, or roll it into an IRA. Cashing it out usually means taxes plus a penalty.
  • High earners: starting in 2026, people who earned over about $150,000 in wages the year before must make catch-up contributions as Roth.

On your own

IRA (Individual Retirement Account)

An account you open yourself at a brokerage, separate from any job. Anyone with earned income can contribute, and a non-working spouse can too through the working spouse.

  • 2026 limit: $7,500 total across all your IRAs, plus $1,100 more at age 50+.
  • Roth IRA: the amount you can put in phases out at higher incomes ($153,000–$168,000 single, $242,000–$252,000 married filing jointly in 2026). You can take out what you contributed (not the growth) anytime without tax or penalty.
  • Traditional IRA: anyone with earned income can contribute, but the tax deduction phases out at higher incomes if you also have a plan at work.
  • Deadline: you can contribute for a year up to the tax filing deadline the next April.

If you're self-employed

  • SEP IRA: simple to set up; you contribute as the employer, up to 25% of compensation (capped at $72,000 in 2026).
  • Solo 401(k): for owners with no employees besides a spouse. You contribute as both employee (up to $24,500) and employer, up to $72,000 total. Often lets you save more than a SEP at lower incomes.
  • SIMPLE IRA: for small businesses with employees; $17,000 limit in 2026 (more for some plans and at age 50+), with a required employer contribution.

The HSA: a health account that doubles for retirement

A Health Savings Account works with a high-deductible health plan. It's the only account that's tax-free going in, while growing, and coming out for medical costs. After 65, you can take money out for anything, taxed like a Traditional IRA.

  • 2026 limit: $4,400 self-only, $8,750 family, plus $1,000 at age 55+.
  • You can invest the balance and save receipts to reimburse yourself years later.
  • Only available if your health plan qualifies; ask HR or your insurer.

Pensions and Social Security

Pensions (defined-benefit plans) pay a set monthly amount for life based on your pay and years of service. They're now rare outside government, teaching and some union jobs. If you have one, find out when you're vested and how the benefit is figured.

Social Security is paid for through payroll taxes. You generally need about 10 years of work (40 credits) to qualify, and the benefit is based on your highest 35 years of earnings.

  • You can start as early as 62, with a permanently smaller check.
  • Full retirement age is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
  • Waiting past full retirement age raises the check about 8% a year, up to age 70.
  • See your own estimate at ssa.gov with a free "my Social Security" account.

A regular brokerage account

A taxable investment account with no special tax break, but also no limits and no early-withdrawal penalties. Growth is taxed when you sell, usually at lower long-term capital gains rates if you held over a year. People often use one after filling their retirement accounts, or for goals before 59½.

What to invest in

  • Target-date funds (like "2055 Fund") pick a stock/bond mix for the year you plan to retire and get more conservative over time. One fund, hands-off.
  • Index funds buy a whole market (like the S&P 500) at very low cost.
  • Fees matter. A fund's expense ratio is charged every year. 0.05% versus 1% can mean tens of thousands of dollars over a career.

Rules to know before you take money out

Age 59½Taking money from a 401(k) or IRA before this usually means income tax plus a 10% penalty. Exceptions include disability, certain medical costs, and up to $10,000 from an IRA for a first home.
Rule of 55If you leave a job in or after the year you turn 55, you can take money from that job's 401(k) without the 10% penalty.
401(k) loansMany plans let you borrow from yourself (up to $50,000 or half your balance). If you leave the job, it may come due quickly.
Roth 5-year ruleRoth growth comes out tax-free once you're 59½ and the account is at least 5 years old.
Required withdrawalsTraditional accounts must start paying out at age 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later). Roth IRAs never require withdrawals during your lifetime.
Saver's CreditLower-income savers can get a tax credit for contributing (2026 income limits: $40,250 single, $60,375 head of household, $80,500 married filing jointly).

A common order to fund things

There's no single right answer, but many personal-finance guides suggest roughly this order. Your situation (job benefits, income, debts, goals) may change it.

  1. Build a small emergency fund so a surprise doesn't land on a credit card.
  2. Contribute enough to get your employer's full match.
  3. Pay off high-interest debt, like credit cards.
  4. Grow the emergency fund toward 3–6 months of expenses.
  5. If you have one, fund an HSA.
  6. Fund a Roth or Traditional IRA.
  7. Go back and raise your workplace plan contributions toward the limit.
  8. Invest more in a regular brokerage account, or put extra toward lower-interest debt.

Rules and limits change, and taxes depend on your situation. For decisions with big tax consequences, a fee-only financial planner or tax professional can help.
